Design, Modeling, and Control of a Coaxial Drone
Liangming Chen , Senior Member , IEEE, Jiaping Xiao , Graduate Student Member , IEEE, Y umin Zheng ,
N Arun Alagappan , and Mir Feroskhan , Member , IEEE
Abstract—Various quadrotor drones have been developed in
recent years, mainly focusing on either improving maximum thrust
per platform area or ﬂight maneuverability. Evidently, achieving
both advantages simultaneously is a challenging task, since they
call for opposing rotor requirements. Speciﬁcally, improving the
drone’s maximum thrust per platform area mainly requires re-
ducing the number of rotors to make way for larger and more
powerful rotors. While this can be an effective method to increase
overall thrust, improving ﬂight maneuverability requires a greater
number of rotors to generate larger rotating torques or to increase
the thrust vectoring capability. To address this challenge, we design
a novel coaxial drone with two contra-rotating rotors for high thrust
efﬁciency while enabling independent dual-axis rotor rotation to
maintain maneuverability along the roll and pitch axes. The thrust
vectoring capability is provided by two dedicated servomotors
connected vertically in series with the coaxial propellers to produce
a compact and elongated fuselage frame. A nonlinear ﬂight model
in six degrees of freedom is developed for the underactuated system,
incorporating four control inputs from the two propellers and
servos, respectively. Consequently, a nonlinear control allocation
approach is proposed such that the drone can produce a desired
control force and yaw torque to stabilize the drone’s position
and yaw angle. For the uncontrolled roll and pitch dynamics, a
damping component is added such that the roll and pitch angular
velocities can also be stabilized. Both numerical simulations and
real experiments are conducted to validate the design of the drone
and the effectiveness of the proposed control strategy.
Index Terms—Coaxial drone, control allocation, drone design,
dynamics modeling, ﬂight control, thrust efﬁciency.

I. I NTRODUCTION
W
ITH the recent technological advancements in aerial
vehicles’ power supply, communication, and onboard
computation, the use of quadrotors in various engineering ap-
plications has become increasingly popular [1]. In particular,
quadrotors can provide increased safety and reduce the cost of
operations in high-risk tasks or environments, such as disaster
management [2], search and rescue [3], bridge and building
inspection [4].
The conventional quadrotor design adopts a plus or cross
conﬁguration with two pairs of counter-rotating propellers con-
nected to a central body. Owe to its mechanically simplicity,
quadrotors enjoy a high stability in hover and a high maneu-
verability in controlled movements involving roll and pitch
motions. However, the maximum thrust per platform area, i.e.,
thrust efﬁciency, for quadrotors is generally limited due to the
presence of four propellers, whose rotor size is proportional to
the central body. Note that the maximum thrust per platform area
is an important index for a drone’s ﬂight duration and power
consumption [5].
Subsequently, this drives the motivation to design a new drone,
which can achieve higher thrust per platform area while still
preserving the same maneuverable degrees of freedom (DoF)
as quadrotors. First, for the thrust efﬁciency, since the power
needed to hover is proportional to the inverse of the rotor’s
radius, a single large rotor will energetically outperform several
smaller rotors within the same boundary [5]. Motivated by this
fact, a triangular quadrotor conﬁguration is proposed in [5] by
using a single large rotor for lift and three small rotors for control.
Also, a bicopter drone is designed in [6], which has similar
levels of versatility and improves the drone’s compactness or
efﬁciency at the same time. Second, to further increase drones’
maneuverable DoF, tilting rotors are employed for the drone
design [7], [8], [9], [10]. For example, an overactuated quadrotor
is developed in [8] based on four tilting rotors. Since coaxial
contra-rotating rotors can provide a compact and anti-torque
solution [11], a coaxial drone is one of the promising options
to simultaneously satisfy these two requirements, i.e., higher
thrust per platform area and the same maneuverable DoF as
quadrotors [12]. The effect of coaxial propellers’ conﬁguration
on propulsion efﬁciency is discussed in [13], which is deﬁned
as the multiplication of the propeller’s efﬁciency, the motor’s
efﬁciency, and the electronic speed controller’s efﬁciency.
Several coaxial drones have been developed in [11], [12],
[14], [15], [16], one of which is the NASA ’s Ingenuity Mars
helicopter, which successfully completed its ﬁrst ﬂight test in
early 2021. The Ingenuity helicopter has contra-rotating coaxial

propellers connected to a lightweight body frame. With a high
thrust efﬁciency, the coaxial design of Ingenuity allows it to
perform basic takeoff and landing maneuvers under the thin at-
mosphere and restrictive energy equipment in Mars1. Unlike the
coaxial quadrotor drone in [16], other coaxial drones designed
in [11], [12], [14], [15] contain coaxial propellers, whose roll
and pitch motions are controlled by their designed mechanical
swashplates. Similar to the coaxial drones in [11], [12], [14],
[15], the roll and pitch motions of the Ingenuity helicopter are
independently controlled by two cyclic swashplates 1, each of
which is driven by three small servomotors. However, the use
of two swashplates with six servomotors involves more intricate
moving parts that increase the overall mechanical complexity of
the platform. As a result, with an increased control complexity
of the drone, the probability of failure is also escalated.
Inspired by existing coaxial drones [11], [12], [14], [15], and
the tilting mechanism [6], [7], [8], [9], [10] employed in drone
design, this article presents a compact drone with contra-rotating
coaxial propellers that is capable of achieving independent bi-
axial rotation about two orthogonal axes. This design preserves
the four maneuvering DoF with a higher thrust per platform
area than that of a conventional quadrotor. Unlike the cyclic
swashplate mechanism used in other coaxial drones, only two
servomotors are directly employed to provide thrust vectoring
capability in producing the roll and pitch motions. This enables
the platform to be controlled in a more intuitive and mechanically
simpler way. Another potential advantage of using servomotors
for the control of roll and pitch motions is their larger outputted
angle range than that of swashplates. Based on the integration
of the coaxial propellers with the two servomotors connected in
series, a detailed six-DoF model involving the drone’s position
and attitude dynamics is developed. Then, to design an efﬁcient
controller for the underactuated system based on the four control
inputs given by the two propellers and two servos, respectively, a
nonlinear control allocation approach is proposed, under which
the drone can produce desired control force and yaw torque to
stabilize the drone’s position and yaw angle. Finally, we validate
our established model and proposed control strategy through
both numerical simulations and real-ﬂight experiments.
The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II
introduces the overall design of the coaxial drone. Section III
establishes the attitude and position dynamics for the coaxial
drone. The control design is provided in Section IV. Sections V
and VI present the simulation results and experimental results,
respectively. Finally, Section VII concludes this article.
II. D ESIGN OF THE COAXIAL DRONE
In this section, we introduce the design of the coaxial drone.
First, we present the drone’s overall mechanical structure and
then introduce different rigid bodies in the coaxial drone and
their respective coordinate frames. Finally, we discuss the con-
nection of the coaxial propeller system, servomotors, and the
drone’s fuselage.

A. Overall Mechanical Structure
The coaxial drone consists of one propeller system, two ser-
vomotors and one lower fuselage section. The coaxial propeller
system consists of two counter-rotating rotors powered by a pair
of brushless motors that generate necessary thrust for ﬂight. The
two servomotors produce localized roll and pitch rotations of
the propeller system for thrust vectoring. A series of connecting
mounts also provide structural support for the coaxial drone.
The drone’s fuselage section houses the ﬂight controller, battery,
and other electrical components. The components located in the
drone’s fuselage have a relatively larger mass compared to the
servomotors and the propeller, resulting in the center of gravity
(CG) of the entire coaxial drone to be located in the fuselage
section. We assume that the CG of the entire drone is vertically
aligned with the CG of the drone’s fuselage. A representation
of the coaxial drone and its localized rotation angles are shown
in Figs. 1 and 2. In addition, since the airﬂow produced by the
propeller system is towards the direction from the propeller to
the fuselage, the fuselage part is designed to be as vertical as
possible, ensuring minimum obstruction to the airﬂow.

C. Connection of Coaxial Propeller , Servomotors, and
Fuselage
The coaxial drone has a simple assembly, with its mechanical
and electrical components connected in a vertical arrangement.
The coaxial propeller system serves as the primary source of
thrust for the drone along a common axis. A carefully designed
rotation mount provides sufﬁcient clearance for the unobstructed
rotation of the propeller byδrφ ∈[−90◦,90◦], as shown in Fig.2.
A stronger rotation mount is used for the secondary servomotor
S2 to support the combined weight of the propeller system
and S1. Similarly, Fig. 2 also shows that a rotation clearance
of δrθ ∈[−90◦,90◦] can be achieved by the subsequent mount
in the system, which indicates that the outputted angle range
of servomotors is larger than that of swashplates. This dual
arrangement of the servomotors allows the drone to perform
forward–backward and lateral maneuvers in ﬂight by rotating
the direction of the generated thrust. The usage of the anglesδrφ
andδrθ to maintain ﬂight stability of the drone is explored in the
follow-up sections of this article.
Another consideration for the assembly of the drone is the de-
sign of the connecting base mount (see the component between
OS2 and Ob in Fig. 1) attached between the end of S2 and the
drone’s fuselage. The dimensions of the base mount are designed
to allow for a secure connection for ease of assembly and
effective transfer of forces. The drone’s fuselage consists of two
housing frames that contain the ﬂight controller and the battery
pack, respectively. All remaining electrical cables and connect-
ing components are stored within the drone’s fuselage as well.
D. Comparison With the Existing Design of Coaxial Drones
The main difference between the coaxial drone introduced
in this article and other birotor coaxial drones developed in
Fig. 3. Comparison between a quadrotor and the coaxial drone.
[11], [15] lies in the mechanism designed to control the thrust
vectoring of the propeller system. In [11], [15], the proposed
coaxial drones utilize a swashplate platform to control their
direction of ﬂight, in which servomotors attached to the platform
change the pitch of the propeller blades at each rotation cycle
and then generate thrust components parallel to the desired
direction. Through this mechanism, thrust vectoring is indirectly
controlled by the servomotors. In [12, Section 2], two servomo-
tors in a parallel mechanism are used to control the tilt of the
swashplate. In [14], a bioinspired reaction wheel mechanism is
employed for the design of a coaxial helicopter. In this article, the
proposed coaxial drone design has two servomotors connected
directly to the propeller system through the use of individual
rotation mounts, under which the parallel coaxial propellers do
not need to tilt with respect to the rotors’ brushless motor to
achieve thrust vectoring. The main advantage of this design is
that it is more intuitive and mechanically simpler in maneuvering
the drone toward the desired ﬂight direction. The increase in
drone height due to the vertical arrangement of the servomotors
is compensated by the compact design of the drone fuselage. The
fuselage is assembled as modular sections, which also reduces
the drone’s weight as opposed to the bulky cylindrical housing,
as shown in [12] and [15]. The usage of swashplates involves
multiple moving parts actuated by servomotors and hence has
a higher probability of failure. For example, the Ingenuity heli-
copter has two swashplates for independent pitch control of each
rotor and is required to perform an automated check on all six
servos before each ﬂight. Another difference of using servomo-
tors to generate the drone’s roll and pitch motions is their larger
outputted angle range than that of swashplates, resulting in a
potential advantage and improved degree of control, as shown
in Fig. 2.

Now, we analyze the properties of the overall dynamics (19),
which will be the foundation for the control design.
1) Underactuated Dynamics: In the overall nonlinear dy-
namics (19), the states [a⊤,p⊤]⊤ ∈R6 are six dimensions.
However, the control input[Ω2
1,Ω2
2,δrθ,δrφ] ∈R4 are only four
dimensions, which implies an underactuated dynamics. There-
fore, we are only able to fully control four states. As similar to
the quadrotor case, the three DoF positions and yaw angle are
usually more of importance in many applications. Therefore, we
can select them as the four controllable states, and ﬁrstly leave
roll and pitch angles as uncontrollable states.
2) Coupling Effect: The attitude dynamics in (19) are inde-
pendent to the position dynamics, while the position dynamics
are coupled with the attitude dynamics due to the existence of
the component m(R ˙a)×M ˙p. Due to the absence of an attitude
damping part in(10), the attitude dynamics are naturally unstable
in the sense that given a nonzero initial states, the attitude
may diverge or oscillate without external control torques. This
can be veriﬁed by linearizing the attitude dynamics around the
equilibrium ˙a =0 . Thus, additional damping components are
needed to stabilize the attitude dynamics. In addition, due to the
existence of the gravity componentF b
g in the position dynamics,
the control force F b
t should not be zero even at a stable state.
3) Model Accuracy: In this model, the drone has been de-
signed such that its CG lies at the center of its fuselage section.
This design speciﬁcation can be achieved by moving the heavier
components in the drone into the fuselage section. In addition,
according to the later simulation and experiments, the drone
keeps almost vertical during hovering and slowly maneuvering
missions, for which the current modeling based on static CG
can be used in many scenarios. While for fast maneuvering
mission, the modeling based on multibody dynamics might be
more appropriate, which will be considered as our future work.

C. Damping Uncontrolled Roll and Pitch Dynamics
After the position and yaw angle of the drone are controlled
to track their desired trajectory and yaw angle, the dynamics of
the roll and pitch angles will be fully uncontrolled and evolve
according to (19). According to (20) and (21), the divergence
of the roll and pitch angular velocities will also make the whole
system divergent since both Tψd and F d will become inﬁnite,
thus making Ω2
1,Ω2
2,δrθ,δrφ also inﬁnite. Therefore, to avoid
this scenario, suppressing the roll and pitch angular velocities is
crucial.
We note that the vibration of the roll and pitch angles of
the drone originates from the actions applied to the two tilt
servomotors. Therefore, a natural solution is to add roll and
pitch damping components into the corresponding servomotors.

according to (29) and (30), we command them by

for which we will tune the gain parameters ﬁrst in our ex-

second-order, the system performance may not be the best if the
gains are too large or small. In addition, the two servomotors’
updating frequency also determines the system performance,
where the difference is that the performance is usually better
given larger updating frequency.
Remark 6: In the proposed controllers (20), (35), and (36),
the angular velocities in roll, pitch, and yaw axes are needed,
which are usually noisy if they are obtained from a pure inertial
measurement unit (IMU). To improve IMUs’ measuring accu-
racy and reduce noise’s magnitude, many auxiliary measure-
ments and techniques have been developed, such as the sensor
fusion based on magnetic measurements and advanced digital
ﬁltering techniques. Therefore, we can get the angular velocities
with relatively small magnitude of noise from these IMUs. In
addition, from the dynamics of the drone, (19) indicates that
the system performance depends on the control laws’ PD gain
parameters, the damping gains, and the updating frequency of
the servomotors, etc. Therefore, by experimental tests, we need
to choose better control gains to mitigate the negative effect of
measurement noises.

VII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this article, we have designed an innovative coaxial drone
such that the advantages of maximum thrust per platform area
and ﬂight maneuverability can be simultaneously actualized
using thrust-vectoring coaxial propellers. The contra-rotating
coaxial propellers can be actuated using a left–right tilt

servomotor and a back–forward tilt servomotor connected
vertically in series. The drone’s position and attitude dynamics
with six DoF have been modeled, which is underactuated
since the number of the control inputs is only four. An efﬁcient
controller with a nonlinear control allocation has been proposed.
For the uncontrolled roll and pitch dynamics, a damping part
has been delicately added such that the roll and pitch angular
velocities can also be stabilized. Both numerical simulations and
real experiments have been conducted, which have validated
the effectiveness of the drone design, the established model and
the proposed control strategy.
An interesting direction for future work is the usage of the
coaxial drone for aerial manipulation. Speciﬁcally, if an one-link
arm is installed at the bottom side of the drone and the other
side of the arm is with adhesive/magnetic mechanisms, then the
coaxial drone can be used to pick up a target and move it to a
destination. In such case, the CG of the combined arm and drone
is shifted downward, as compared with the original drone. If the
connection between the arm and the target is rigid, then the arm
and the target can be regarded as a part of the drone’s fuselage
part, in which our proposed control design still applies. For the
case of a two-link arm, if the two links are not vertical, then
the assumption regarding the CG of the whole structure may be
violated. An alternative for such case is to use the Lagrangian
approach to model the dynamics of the drone. Speciﬁcally, we
need to calculate the kinetic and potential energy of the drone
consisting of multibody components. However, the established
Lagrangian model will be quite nonlinear, whose control design
usually needs the usage of the tool of feedback linearization.
Another possible alternative is via coordinating two such coaxial
drones for aerial manipulation tasks, in which the positions of the
two coaxial drones are symmetric with respect to the geometric
center of the whole combined system, in which the control
design for such system can be handled based on the symmetry
of this system and the control design for single coaxial drone.
